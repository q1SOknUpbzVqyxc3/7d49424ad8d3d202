Landing pages that keep the ad's promise

The user arrives believing something specific, because your ad told them so. Whether the next screen confirms or contradicts that belief decides most of your conversion rate.

The gap that costs the most

Ads are produced by people optimising for attention. Pages are produced by people optimising for completeness. The result is a specific promise followed by a general page, and a measurable drop between click and first meaningful action. It looks like traffic quality in the report — the media team is asked to fix it, and they cannot, because it is not a media problem.

The test is simple and uncomfortable: cover the logo, read the ad, then read the first screen of the page. If a stranger cannot tell they belong together, the ad is paying for a handover that fails.

Match four things, in order

  1. The claim. The specific promise from the ad, in comparable words, above the fold. Not a paraphrase — the same claim.
  2. The proof. Whatever made the claim credible in the ad — a number, a demonstration, a testimonial — should appear within the first screen.
  3. The visual. Same product state, same colour, same person if the ad featured one. Recognition happens before reading.
  4. The action. One primary action matching the ad's implied next step. A page offering three equally weighted options converts worse than one that assumes.

One page per angle beats one excellent page for all angles, almost every time.

Friction is measurable, so measure it

Before redesigning anything, instrument the path: page view, scroll depth, first field interaction, field-level abandonment, submit attempt, error shown, completion. Most pages have one field or one step responsible for a disproportionate share of loss, and it is rarely the one the team suspects. Field-level data turns a redesign argument into a fix.

Two structural offenders are worth checking first: fields you do not need at this stage, and validation that punishes the user after submission rather than guiding them during entry. Both are cheap to fix and both are common.

Mobile is the page, not a version of it

In most paid social accounts, the overwhelming majority of traffic is mobile, which means the mobile experience is the page and the desktop one is the adaptation. Load time on a mid-range device over a mobile network — not on the designer's laptop — is a conversion metric. So are tap target size, keyboard type per field, and whether the primary action is reachable with a thumb.

Test with enough traffic to get an answer

The most common CRO failure is running an underpowered test, declaring a two percent lift, shipping it, and building the next quarter on noise. Calculate the sample size first. If the traffic is not there, do not run a subtle test — run a bold one. Small changes need large samples; large changes can be judged with what you have.

And when a page wins, feed it back to the creative team. Message match is a loop: the page that converts tells you which claim survives contact with a decision, which is exactly what the next batch of ads should be built on.

Takeaways

  • Match claim, proof, visual and action between ad and page — in that order.
  • Instrument field-level drop-off before redesigning anything.
  • Size tests properly; if traffic is thin, test bold changes rather than subtle ones.
